At-Home Skyrizi Induction Approved for Crohn's After 56% Reached Clinical Remission at 12 Weeks Versus 30% on Placebo

Adults with moderately to severely active Crohn's disease can now start Skyrizi at home instead of receiving IV infusions. The FDA approved an induction regimen given under the skin with an on-body injector, AbbVie announced October 23.

The approval was based on the AFFIRM trial, in which 56% of patients on the at-home regimen reached clinical remission at week 12, compared with 30% on placebo, according to AbbVie's announcement of the approval. Endoscopic response, a measure of reduced inflammation seen in the bowel, was 43% versus 15%.

For patients, the change is mostly about access. Starting treatment without repeated infusion center visits can mean less travel and fewer missed workdays, especially for people far from a specialty center. Crohn's disease is usually diagnosed in people ages 20 to 30, when work and family demands can make scheduling infusions difficult.

Inside the AFFIRM Results

AFFIRM was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial with 274 patients. Twice as many received risankizumab, the drug in Skyrizi, as received placebo. The treatment beat placebo on both main goals: clinical remission and endoscopic response at week 12.

The trial population was hard to treat. About 65% had already failed at least one advanced therapy, such as another biologic drug, HCPLive reported.

Results differed by group. Among patients who had never tried an advanced therapy, 74% reached clinical remission, compared with 27% on placebo. Among those who had failed one before, the rate was 45% versus 31%, a narrower gap. The group new to advanced therapy was small, with 65 patients on the drug, so those results are less certain than the overall findings.

Endoscopic remission, a stricter measure of healing, was 26% versus 11% overall. The drug also improved fatigue scores at week 12.

Clinical Remission, Explained

Clinical remission in this trial meant a Crohn's Disease Activity Index score below 150. That score combines symptoms such as stool frequency and abdominal pain with general well-being and other factors.

Endoscopic response is different. It required a drop of more than 50% in a score of inflammation seen during a camera exam of the bowel, as judged by a central reviewer. This matters because feeling better does not always mean the bowel has healed.

These are 12-week induction results. The trial did not directly compare at-home induction with IV induction, so it cannot show whether one route works better than the other.

Dosing, Safety and the Company's Claims

The new regimen is 720 mg given under the skin at weeks 0, 2, 4, and 8. Patients then continue with maintenance doses every eight weeks starting at week 12. The IV induction option, 600 mg at weeks 0, 4 and 8, remains available.

AbbVie says the safety profile of at-home induction was generally consistent with what is known about Skyrizi in Crohn's disease, with no new safety risks seen. That conclusion comes from the company's summary of the trial.

The Skyrizi prescribing information includes warnings for serious allergic reactions, infections, and liver problems. For Crohn's disease, it calls for checking liver enzymes and bilirubin before treatment and during induction for at least the first 12 weeks. An updated label now tells doctors to consider tuberculosis testing before starting treatment based on clinical judgment, rather than requiring it for every patient.

Patients should contact their doctor for fever, chills, a persistent cough, or other signs of infection, and should not skip scheduled liver blood tests. Yellowing skin or eyes, dark urine or pain in the upper right belly could signal a liver problem and need prompt medical attention. Trouble breathing or facial swelling after a dose requires emergency care.

Dr. Millie Long, chief of the division of gastroenterology and hepatology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, led the trial, so her view is not independent. "AFFIRM answered the first question, with superior rates of clinical remission and endoscopic response versus placebo during induction with subcutaneous SKYRIZI, particularly in patients new to advanced therapy," she said in AbbVie's release.

AbbVie also cites a company survey of 352 adults with inflammatory bowel disease in which 87% were satisfied with the on-body injector. Its comparison with an autoinjector is listed as company data on file and cannot be independently assessed, BioPharm International noted. Earlier IV induction trials showed week-12 remission rates of 45% and 42%, though those trials enrolled different patient populations.

Cost and coverage were not addressed. Biologic drugs usually require prior authorization, so patients should ask their insurer whether at-home induction is covered and whether a specialty pharmacy is required. Patients already doing well on IV induction or other drugs should not switch without talking with their gastroenterologist. Those starting treatment can ask who will train them on the injector, what to do if a dose fails to deliver, and when results will be checked with blood tests or a scope.

Key Questions Answered

What did the FDA approve?

An at-home induction regimen of Skyrizi, given under the skin with an on-body injector, for adults with moderately to severely active Crohn's disease.

How well did it work?

In the AFFIRM trial, 56% reached clinical remission at 12 weeks versus 30% on placebo, and 43% had an endoscopic response versus 15%.

What is clinical remission?

A Crohn's Disease Activity Index score below 150, reflecting few or mild symptoms.

Were patients hard to treat?

Yes. About 65% had already failed an advanced therapy.

What are the main safety warnings?

Serious allergic reactions, infections, and liver problems. Liver tests are recommended before and during induction.

Is IV induction still available?

Yes. Patients and doctors can choose IV or at-home induction.
