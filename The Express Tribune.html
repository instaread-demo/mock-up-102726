Building trust in the age of AI: The ethics of digital banking

Mobilink Bank prioritises ethical AI, strong governance and customer trust as digital banking evolves

Haaris M Chaudhary | October 21, 2026

In banking, trust is not simply part of the business; it is the foundation on which the entire relationship with a customer rests.

Digital banking has changed how that trust is earned, tested, and how quickly it can be lost. Today, customers can access banking services from the comfort of their homes, while AI is increasingly shaping their experiences, from personalised services to automated financial decisions.

These advances create immense opportunities, but they also bring greater responsibility. As financial institutions embrace new technologies, we must remain mindful of how we use them, how we protect the information entrusted to us, and how we ensure that innovation continues to serve our customers. This is why I see ethics and governance as integral to how a bank operates and grows.

In addition to shaping decisions, good governance is also about guiding how risks are assessed and accountability is assigned. In a digital banking environment, where decisions are executed at enormous speed and scale, governance cannot be neglected or left behind in the pursuit of innovation.

Mobilink Bank’s customer-first approach provides us with a useful test for these decisions. We always ask ourselves whether the financial solutions we are creating genuinely address customer needs, whether our customers understand what they are signing up for, and whether our technology-based solutions protect their interests.

We recognise that digital banking can extend formal financial services to people and businesses that previously had limited access to them. However, we also understand that the true value of digital banking depends on the experiences it delivers and the outcomes it creates for these customers.

AI undoubtedly has considerable potential in banking, from detecting fraud more effectively and analysing information faster to improving customer service, strengthening risk management, and automating repetitive tasks in lengthy processes that could otherwise consume considerable employee time; however, it also adds another dimension to responsibility.

I believe the real opportunity lies in harnessing AI to improve banking outcomes while ensuring that its use remains transparent and accountable. We at Mobilink Bank take a risk-based approach to AI adoption, distinguishing between processes that can be automated with appropriate controls and decisions that require stronger oversight.

Processes with clearly defined parameters and lower risks offer greater scope for automation, improving operational efficiency and team performance. However, decisions involving credit, customer privacy, financial security, or other areas with significant consequences require stronger oversight, validation, and human judgment. Technology can certainly assist and facilitate decision-making, but we must be clear about who ultimately holds that accountability.

If we know what we are trying to improve through AI, we can focus on the outcomes rather than the technology itself. With that clarity, we can make informed decisions about how systems are designed. Privacy, information security, fairness, and governance become far more effective when they are built into the architecture from the beginning.

Beyond systems and controls, there is a human dimension to ethical banking that requires equal attention. Policies and controls help establish boundaries; however, it is people who operate and make decisions within those boundaries every day.

This places a particular responsibility on leaders to ensure that employees feel comfortable raising concerns, questioning decisions they believe carry risks, and exercising their judgment, even when there is pressure to move quickly or meet business targets. Ultimately, an organisation’s culture is reflected in how it responds when doing the right thing becomes difficult.

For me, responsible AI also means looking at the outcomes these systems create for different customers. In banking, a decision can influence someone's access to finance, their ability to grow a business, or their confidence in the institution serving them.

We must therefore look beyond whether a system works efficiently and ask whether it works fairly, whether its decisions can be appropriately reviewed, and whether it is delivering the outcomes we intended. That is an important part of ensuring that innovation serves customers rather than expecting customers to adapt to technology.

On Global Ethics Day, I believe the principle is simple: technology will continue to evolve, and so will the way we deliver financial services. But our responsibility to the people who trust us must remain central to how we make decisions, adopt new technologies, and define progress.
