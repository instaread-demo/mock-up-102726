    In France, the youth has just started to rise up

    Listen to audio version of this article
    Sponsored by
    0:00 / 6:21
    Instaread
    Between two major protests, our reporter headed to Paris to understand the reasons behind the largest demonstrations the country experienced in decades — and why it won’t easily fade.
    Graffiti on Monument de la Republique, Paris, Oct. 19. (Kay Roxby)

    By Federica Di Sario

    Federica Di Sario is a reporter at The Parliament Magazine.

    26 Oct 2026

    @fed_disario

    Share this page

    PARIS — When I arrived at Place de la République last Wednesday, an almost surreal calm hung over the square, one of the epicenters of the largest anti-government protests in decades. Young families played with old-fashioned wooden games, street vendors sold soft drinks, and groups of friends chatted gleefully. 

    It was only on second glance that I realized that, just like me, many people seemed drawn to the statue of Marianne — not simply to look at it, but to take pictures and videos. To document what they saw. Many were international reporters. 

    Traces of last Saturday’s mass youth protests remained visible on the statue, the personification of the French Republic, its surface coated in fluorescent graffiti calling on the youth to rise up. Below, a sign surrounded by flowers read: “To the dead and wounded of the repression.”

    On Oct. 17, tens of thousands of people took to the streets of Paris to demand greater investment in education to address staff shortages and decrepit infrastructure, as well as urgent action to tackle a cost-of-living crisis that has pushed poverty and inequality to record levels.

    Most demonstrations unfolded peacefully, but violent groups ended up overshadowing them. Rioters set fire to cars and barricades, turning parts of Paris and other French cities into scenes of widespread destruction. Authorities said nearly 1,000 police officers were injured and about 3,000 shops were looted or damaged during the Oct. 17 protests.

    I looked around and I decided to take a chance on a woman in her twenties sitting on a bench. “Are you part of the movement?” I asked. “Of course,” she smiled back. Bingo.

    Coralie, a 23-year-old master’s student in mathematics at Sorbonne Paris Nord University, said she joined the movement just three weeks ago, after high school students came to her university to ask for solidarity. For her, it felt like a no-brainer.

    “They ask us to pay more and more for less and less quality,” she said of her university. “Sometimes there are no chairs, there’s no heating in winter nor AC in summer. We don’t find internships, we’re stuck at the university and we can’t find jobs.” 

    “Everyone is really tired of this situation,” she said.

    On paper, France invests heavily in education, devoting 5.1% of its gross domestic product (GDP) to the sector, well above the OECD average. Yet students and parents alike are left wondering where all that money goes. 

    “There’s definitely a lack of teachers in the lycée,” Firdous, a 36-year-old mother of three I met on the square, told me. “There are a lot of professors who are absent and there’s no replacement for that.” She knows this firsthand, as one of her sons is a teenager. But when I asked whether he was taking part in the protests, she shook her head. “I didn’t let him go. It’s too dangerous.”

    ‘Could you please answer?’

    It all started in mid-September at the Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil, a suburb in southeastern Paris, where students blockaded the building, asking for better education conditions. They have since snowballed in more than 40 cities, and broadened to parents, teachers and trade unions lamenting worsening living conditions. 

    But instead of addressing the students’ key concerns, President Emmanuel Macron used a televised address following the riots of October 17 to condemn the violence and looting, warning that those responsible would face tough penalties.

    “We’ve all wondered: How can French people do this to France?” he asked. “ Nothing justifies it, nothing explains it, nothing excuses it.” 

    Only later did Macron acknowledge that some of the protesters’ concerns were legitimate, largely ascribing the cost-of-living crisis to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz — a war, he stressed, that France had no part in.

    But young protesters find this argument a convenient excuse, pointing out Macron has long refused to engage in a genuine dialogue. 

    “It didn’t meet any of our expectations,” said Edgar Degroodt, a 25-year-old engineering researcher at the Paris hospital network. “With the cost-of-living crisis worsening and little investment in the social and cultural sectors, Macron’s only answer was that it wasn’t his fault, but the result of the war in Iran. That may explain rising fuel prices, but it doesn’t justify everything else.”

    For Coralie, Macron’s reluctance to take the movement seriously was precisely why the demonstrations continued longer than many had expected.

    “We had a lot of questions. At first, all we wanted were answers from the government. Can you do something? If so, what can you do?” she said. “Could you please answer?”

    Last Friday, student representatives were received by Macron, saying he promised to increase the number of teachers and a redesign of the state-run higher education admission platform, Parcoursup, which the movement had slammed as a “social sorting machine.” 

    An unrest fueling the extremes? 

    How to handle a major student and trade union uprising would always have been a political headache for the government. But with the protests unfolding just six months ahead of what some analysts have described as the most consequential presidential election since World War II, their outcome will inevitably reverberate at the ballot box.

    Macron has sought to get a grip on the protests, warning that public anger is being weaponized by political extremists — from the far-left France Unbowed party, whose leader, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, has publicly backed the movement, to the National Rally, which promised to restore law and order. For now, though, no major change in voter preferences was detected by the latest poll conducted in the aftermath of the riots, contrary to expectations that a rise in violence would translate into a boost for Marine Le Pen’s party (RN). 

    The students I talked to, however, were clear that their actions won’t easily be captured by political parties. 

    For Morgan Vaterkowski, a 27-year-old PhD student at Sorbonne University, the main result is that, for the first time in his own life, he feels called to head to the ballot box. “I’ll vote for the first time,” said Vaterkowski. He doesn’t yet know for whom, he told me, but as a researcher, he intends to study each manifesto carefully.
